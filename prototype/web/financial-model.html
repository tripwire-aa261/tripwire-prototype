    Assumptions to question before trusting any number here

    Not in the model (assumptions to keep working on)

    • Every customer of a type is the same. A facility pays in proportion to the sensors it needs against Hays: prisons and jails 1×, large stadiums 1.5×, airports and bases 4×. Within a type, every site is the same size and willing to pay the same. In reality stadiums need different numbers of sensors, and venues with big concerts, playoffs or other large events would likely pay more (pricing per event or by tier). We test this with design partners.
    • No defense programs. Military bases (450, Big Idea Memo) are counted only as fixed sites like airports, from Series B. Defense procurement of counter-drone systems, kits that troops carry, and sales to allied militaries are not in the model.
    • Volume discounts only on our own board. The board price falls with volume (parts list); the computer, antennas, enclosure and bladeRF radios keep today's prices, and the cost of installing and running a facility does not fall as we grow. Supplier quotes at Seed replace these.
    • Abroad looks like the US. Sites abroad are Hays-size prisons and jails at the US price and US installation and upkeep cost; only staff cost less (sliders above). European prices, labor and import costs are not yet researched.
    • One product only: the facility subscription. Not counted: the airspace-map data feed, selling sensors or software to integrators or other vendors, installation and consulting services, taking down drones (which we do not do and which needs legal authority), and other site types such as data centers (3,068 sites, Big Idea Memo).

    The sales funnel the plan needs

    How many organizations we must be talking to each year to sign the customers the plan assumes. A lead becomes a customer 1 time in 10, a proposal 1 in 3, a negotiation 1 in 2 (the AA261 class example: 10 leads, 3 prospects, 2 negotiations and 1 signature over four quarters), and a sale takes the sales-cycle slider to close. A customer signs for all its facilities at $25,000 to install plus 5 years of subscription, larger facilities in proportion.

    What investors get back if the company is sold

    Venture investors are paid when the company is sold, not from its profits. Pick a sale date and a price as a multiple of the last four quarters of sales; the company's cash is added to the price. Each round buys a share at the US median valuation for its stage (PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor, Q3 2026: pre-money $18.3M Seed, $60.3M Series A, $187.0M Series B; pre-seed $5M post-money is our assumption), and later rounds shrink earlier shares. Rounds after the sale date are left out. Employee stock options and investor preferences are ignored, so real returns are a little lower.

    In class, investors expect a sale 6 to 8 years after they invest (AA261 lectures); PitchBook reports seven to nine years from founding to a sale or listing.
    Recent prices: DroneShield trades near 4.4 times its 2025 sales; AeroVironment paid about 4.5 times sales for BlueHalo; Motorola paid about 9.3 times for Silvus.
    100% = the median valuation for each stage. A company with little revenue may raise below it.

    How the model works

    Steps, fixed values, what is left out

    Step by step

    • Two design partners, Hays State Prison and Clemson Memorial Stadium, run paid one-year pilots from 2028 Q1. They become the first customers when the product is ready.
    • Each salesperson wins customers; a win becomes a signed contract one "time to close a sale" later, never before the product is ready. Until Series A the CEO sells half time and one account executive full time.
    • A customer (a state prison system, a county, a venue owner) installs its facilities one after another. A facility pays an installation fee once and a subscription every year.
    • Facilities installed can never exceed the facilities that could buy. Each quarter's sales are split across site types (prisons and jails, stadiums, airports and bases) in proportion to what each type still has unsold, and each facility keeps its own type's size and price for as long as it is a customer.
    • Hardware is paid for a quarter before it ships and customers pay a quarter after installation, so cash lags behind profit while the company grows.
    • Cash needed from investors is the most the business is ever short, added up from the start, before counting any investment.

    Fixed values (no slider)

      Left out

      • Grants (SBIR, FEMA): upside, not counted.
      • Tax, interest, replacing old sensors, and how much of the company investors end up owning.
      • Hiring does not slow down if sales disappoint, so the weak scenarios overstate cost.
      • Differences in size and willingness to pay within a customer type, defense programs, and any product other than the facility subscription (see "Not in the model" above).
