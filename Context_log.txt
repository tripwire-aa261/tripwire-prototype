I read all three papers and checked market sizes for the drone and aviation uses they fit. All three come from Akshay Gadre’s lab at the University of Washington and build on the same base, so I treated them as one technology platform.

What the technology actually does
MulDar: Syncs several cheap, off-the-shelf 77 GHz car radar chips (TI AWR2243) using the direct radio path between them, so they work as one radar seeing from several angles. This fixes a basic weakness of single radars: flat or shiny surfaces (truck sides, glass, smooth drone bodies) bounce the signal away from the radar and vanish from view. It also removes “ghost” objects caused by signals bouncing off several surfaces. It cut point-cloud error by 67%.
POLySight: Uses the same sync plus polarization to tell what a surface is made of. It classified 23 solids at 95.7% accuracy, 8 liquids at 96.5% and 4 powders at 100%. It also measured sugar concentration in water to within about 1.5%.
TwinFocus: Fixes the blur that comes from moving a radar by hand. It compares a reference object in the scene against a simple digital model of it, so you get sharp images without a precise mechanical scanner. A drone has the same imprecise-motion problem, so this matters for drone-mounted imaging.
Five ideas
1. Counter-drone sensing from a network of cheap radars (MulDar)

Problem: Small drones are hard for a single radar to see. They are small, partly made of flat plastic or carbon panels, and fly low in clutter.
Approach: Many cheap synced radar nodes around a site catch the energy a single radar misses, and the ghost removal helps in cluttered industrial or urban settings.
Market: Counter-drone systems are about $9.2B in 2026, forecast to reach $29.7B by 2031. Drone detection alone is $0.66B (2024) growing to $2.33B (2029).
Wedge: Short-range protection for prisons, stadiums, substations and forward military bases, where many cheap sensors can beat one expensive radar.
Competition: Echodyne just won a contract worth up to $250M from the Pentagon’s counter-drone task force (Oct 2026), so buyers are spending.
2. Wide-baseline obstacle sensing built into one airframe (MulDar) — eVTOLs, large cargo drones, helicopters

Approach: Put radars on wingtips, booms or rotor arms. They are bolted to the same frame and can always see each other, which removes MulDar’s biggest deployment limit.
Problem it solves: Seeing glass buildings, rooftops and vehicles when landing in fog, dust or brownout, where cameras and lidar fail.
Market: Drone detect-and-avoid is roughly $2.8B (2025) to $10.1B (2034), from a lower-quality report. The FAA’s coming rule for flights beyond visual line of sight (Part 108) is the demand driver.
Defense twin: Helicopter landings in brownout.
3. Drone-mounted radar inspection that sees through coatings (TwinFocus + POLySight)

Approach: mmWave passes through paint, foam insulation, fiberglass and composites. A drone could image corrosion under insulation on pipes, wind turbine blades, or composite aircraft skins in a hangar. TwinFocus corrects for drone wobble using a known feature such as a flange or a marker card.
Market: This is the largest pool. Drone inspection services were about $11.6B in 2022, heading to $23B by 2027. Aerospace non-destructive testing is about $2.0B (2025), heading to $3.65B (2034).
Gap: Today’s drone inspectors for aircraft (e.g. Donecle, Mainblades) only look at the surface.
Risk: TwinFocus was only tested handheld at tens of centimeters. Drone vibration is untested and is the main technical risk.
4. Airfield surface monitoring (MulDar + POLySight)

Approach: Synced radars along the runway edge detect debris and classify what is on the runway: water, ice or slush. Runway condition reporting is still largely done by eye.
Problem: Debris costs aviation about $13B a year.
Market: The debris-detection market itself is only about $46.5M (2024) to $75M (2031). I’d use this as an entry customer or a defense airfield contract, not as the whole company.
5. Portable screening for air cargo and checkpoints (TwinFocus + POLySight)

Approach: A handheld scanner that images through packaging and also says whether the contents are liquid, powder or solid. Those are exactly the categories aviation security restricts.
Market: Air cargo screening was over $1B in 2022, growing about 5% a year.
Risk: Security certification (TSA/ECAC) is slow and expensive.
How mature this is
Lab stage: All three systems ran on TI evaluation boards with a PC. Nothing is flight-ready.
Slow scans: POLySight needs a motion stage and about 2 hours per scan.
Flat surfaces only: POLySight assumes flat surfaces.
Short range: The setups were tested at meters, and 77 GHz radar is short-range by nature. You’d need to prove useful range early, especially for idea 1.

Why battery-free sensing fits aerospace
Aircraft have three problems that battery-free sensors address:

Wiring is heavy and expensive. A Boeing 747 carries about 228 km of wire, roughly 1,600 kg. The A380’s wiring problems are linked to delays costing about $2B.
Batteries are a problem on aircraft. Lithium cells are regulated hazardous material, and replacing thousands of sensor batteries hidden inside an airframe isn’t practical.
Places where electronics can’t survive or wires can’t reach. Engine hot sections, rotating parts, fuel tanks, and sealed composite structures.
One caveat on “ambient” waves. An airframe is mostly metal and blocks outside signals. Inside the engine or the structure, there is little reliable Wi-Fi or cellular signal to harvest. Truly ambient designs work best on the ground: airports, hangars, and cities where delivery drones fly. On the aircraft itself, practical systems usually use a dedicated reader that powers the tags and reads their reflected signal (backscatter).

Six ideas
1. Wireless structural sensors read during maintenance (best near-term option)

Approach: Stick or embed battery-free strain, crack, corrosion or moisture sensors in hidden, corrosion-prone areas, such as floor beams under galleys and lavatories, and composite repairs. A mechanic or a drone reads them during inspection instead of removing panels.
Why it’s a good entry: The sensors don’t transmit in flight. That avoids most flight certification at first and saves inspection labor.
Market: Structural health monitoring overall is about $2.7B (2025), forecast at $6.7B by 2034. Aerospace isn’t broken out separately. Aerospace inspection testing adds about $2.0B.
Defense twin: Keeping aging military fleets flying is a large, steady budget.
2. Drones that read a field of battery-free sensors (connects to your radar work)

Approach: Embed thousands of cheap tags in bridges, pipelines, solar farms or fields. A drone flies over, powers them and reads them. The drone is the reader, so the sensors never need batteries.
Link to your radar platform: mmWave tags that reflect a signal back can also serve as landing markers that radar sees in fog, for vertiports or military landing zones. The same synced radar nodes from MulDar could image the scene and read the tags.
Why it’s strong: “See the scene and read the sensors with one cheap radar network” is a hard combination to copy.
Market: Taps the drone inspection services market (~$23B by 2027) cited earlier.
3. Sensors for engines and rotating parts

Approach: Passive surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors have no chip and no battery, so they survive hot-section temperatures. They can also report from spinning parts without slip rings: turbine temperature, helicopter gearbox health, bearing wear.
Trade-off: Very high value, but long certification and established specialists. Best done as a partnership with an engine maker or a big supplier, not as a solo startup.
4. Cabin compliance sensing

Approach: Battery-free tags that confirm each life vest is present, seatbelts are fastened, and galley equipment is latched, replacing manual checks.
Market: Sits within aviation RFID, about $1.2B (2025) to $2.7B (2034). Maintenance is about 12% of that market.
Risk: Plain ID tagging is commoditized. You’d only stand out if your tags measure something, not just identify an item.
5. Spacecraft and launch vehicles

Approach: Every kilogram of harness costs launch mass, and you can’t send a technician to change a battery in orbit. Battery-free sensors could monitor structure and temperature on satellites and rocket stages.
Market: Smaller, but customers pay premium prices, and NASA and the Space Force fund early work.
6. Defense logistics condition monitoring

Approach: Battery-free tags that record temperature, humidity and shock history on stored spare parts and sensitive cargo for years, with no batteries to fail on the shelf.
Watch-outs
Range: RF-powered backscatter typically reads from centimeters to a few meters. That shapes the product: handheld or drone readers, not one reader covering a whole aircraft.
Certification and radio interference: Anything that transmits on an aircraft goes through environmental and interference testing (DO-160). The band reserved for wireless avionics, 4.2–4.4 GHz, is shared with radio altimeters. That band was at the center of the 5G C-band interference fight, so expect regulators to be cautious.
Metal detunes tags. Tags on aluminum need special antenna design. Composites are friendlier.
My recommendation
Combine this with the radar work instead of starting a separate company.

Product: Sell the synced radar network as both the imager and the reader for cheap battery-free tags.
First market: Maintenance inspection (idea 1), because it avoids flight certification.
Drone extension: Drone-read sensor fields and radar-visible landing markers (idea 2).
That gives you one technology base with both defense and commercial customers.

Short answer
Expect roughly 40–100 m per cheap node against a small consumer drone like a DJI Mavic or Phantom. An engineered node can reach about 150–300 m. Networking several nodes together helps less than you might hope: it mainly makes detection more reliable and covers more area. It does not get you kilometre-class range. For that you’d still need a lower-frequency, higher-power radar like Echodyne’s or Robin’s to give early warning.

Why range grows so slowly
Radar detection range grows with only the fourth root of everything you improve: transmit power, antenna gain, integration time, or the target’s radar cross-section (RCS, how big it looks to radar). To double range you need 16× more signal.

A small quadcopter is a tiny target at 77 GHz. Measurements put a DJI Phantom 3 at about −20 dBsm, or 0.01 m². I ran the standard radar equation for TI-class hardware and checked it against published tests:

Setup	Estimated range	Real-world check
Single stock chip (AWR2243-class), normal frame	~35 m	
Same, listening longer (~50 ms) on a hovering drone	~60 m	TI IWR6843 detected a 250 g drone at 75–80 m. A research system detected an 8 kg hexacopter at 80 m, a limit set by its configuration, not its sensitivity.
4-chip cascaded board (12 transmit / 16 receive antennas, the board TwinFocus used)	~160 m	
Single chip + high-gain lens horns	~300 m	St Andrews’ FAROS-E: Phantom detected at ~300 m, classified at ~150 m
Bigger drone (−10 dBsm), stock chip	~100 m	
Group 3-class drone (~0 dBsm), stock chip	~180 m	
The catch with high-gain antennas is a very narrow beam, about 2° wide. You then have to scan mechanically or use many nodes to cover the sky.

What networking the radars adds
How much the network extends range depends on how the nodes’ signals are combined. These figures assume the ~60 m single-node case:

2 nodes: ~63 m added loosely; ~70 m combining receivers in sync; ~80 m combining every transmit–receive pair in sync.
4 nodes: ~70 m loosely; ~80 m with receivers in sync; ~115 m with every pair in sync.
8 nodes: ~75 m loosely; ~100 m with receivers in sync; ~165 m with every pair in sync.
Combining every pair only reaches the top figures if the drone sits in every node’s beam and timing between nodes is accurate to a fraction of the 4 mm wavelength. MulDar’s sync over the direct radio path between radars is what makes this possible in principle. In practice you land between the middle and top figures.

The bigger benefits are elsewhere:

Fewer missed detections. A drone’s radar return swings by 10–20 dB as it turns. Viewing it from several angles at once smooths out those fades, which is the same effect MulDar shows on flat, mirror-like surfaces.
Cleaner tracks in clutter. Removing ghost targets matters most at stadiums, substations and cities.
Coverage grows linearly. Each node you add covers more area, even though range barely moves.
What this means for the product
Node spacing: Plan a mesh with nodes about 50–150 m apart. A prison with a ~1.5 km perimeter needs roughly 15–30 nodes, consistent with the 25 nodes/site assumption in your workbook.
Regulation isn’t the range limit: The FCC allows up to 50 dBm average and 55 dBm peak power (EIRP) at 77 GHz. A stock chip runs at roughly 22 dBm, so there is about 28 dB of legal headroom, at airports at least. What holds range back is cost and beam width, not the rules.
Weather: Heavy rain costs roughly 10 dB/km at 77 GHz. Over a 300 m round trip that cuts range by about 30%. At 50–100 m it barely matters.
Positioning: Pitch this as the inner, clutter-robust layer that is cheap per square metre. Pair it with RF detection or a long-range radar for cueing at a kilometre or more, rather than competing on standoff range.

Three things that affect cost
Cheap ready-made modules don’t work. Modules like the Nanoradar MR72 (about $320–340) output only processed detections, not the raw signal data. MulDar-style sync needs raw data and a hardware trigger pin, which limits you to TI-class chips or a custom board.

Installation will dominate at scale. Installation costs more than the radar itself at scale, so put nodes on existing light poles or runway edge lights where you can.

You’re right to push on this. On cost, the honest answer is that a cheap-radar network does not beat Echodyne for general drone protection. It wins only in a specific niche.

On open ground, Echodyne wins on cost, range and regulatory status. Physics drives the gap: coverage area grows with range squared, and range grows only with the fourth root of radar performance. Cheaper nodes at volume won’t close a 10–15× gap.

Where the network does have an advantage
The question isn’t range. It’s how many viewpoints a site needs.

Cluttered, shadowed sites. Prison cell blocks, stadium bowls, substation steelwork, data-centre rooftops and city blocks all block line of sight. A drone dropping contraband at a cell window, or hovering below a roofline, is hidden from a radar on a pole. These sites need many viewpoints. One extra viewpoint costs ~$40k with Echodyne and ~$2–4k with a node, so the network is about 10× cheaper per viewpoint.

I read the SARLink paper (Stanford/Microsoft, 2025). One correction first: it’s about communication, not detecting objects. A ground reflector with servo motors switches its radar cross-section on and off. That sends bits to a radar satellite (Sentinel-1, 5.4 GHz) that the authors don’t control. It achieved a 10 dB on/off contrast with 50 ms switching, which works out to about 60 bits per satellite pass from a 5.5 ft reflector.

Two ideas in it transfer directly to drone detection:

A modulated reflection stands out from clutter. A target that switches on and off in a known pattern can be picked out even against busy background returns.
Someone else’s radar can be the transmitter. That is the same idea as using cell towers as illuminators.
The satellite link itself is no use for drones, since a satellite passes only once every few days.

Can electrically modulated tags detect drones?
Yes. Electronic switching (a PIN diode or RF switch) runs at kHz–MHz instead of 20 Hz. It draws microwatts, so a small solar cell can power it with no battery. Each tag reflects an illuminator’s signal with its own code or modulation frequency. A receiver can then separate every tag-to-receiver link from static clutter, which is the hardest problem for drone detection near the ground.

A drone shows up on a link in three ways:

Shadowing. A drone crossing a link blocks it. In this crossing (forward-scatter) geometry it looks 100–1000× larger than it does to a normal radar.
Propeller signature. The spinning blades put a micro-Doppler pattern onto the tag’s signal, which helps tell drones from birds.
Extra reflections near the link.
With dozens of tags and a few receivers, you get hundreds of crossing links. A known technique called radio tomographic imaging, so far used mostly to track people indoors, could then locate the drone in 3D.

Band choice (rough numbers for a Mavic-sized drone and a 100 m link):

Band	How much larger the drone looks when blocking a link	Width of the detection zone around each link
2.4 GHz	~23 dB (~200×)	~1.8 m
5.8 GHz	~31 dB	~1.1 m
24 GHz	~43 dB	~0.6 m
77 GHz	~53 dB	~0.3 m
Sub-6 GHz is the sweet spot for a tag mesh, because each link is forgiving and ~1–2 m thick. 77 GHz gives huge gain but razor-thin links, so keep it for precision spots.

My rough link budget gives about 100–200 m from each receiver to its tags at 2.4/5.8 GHz.

Good catch. The “anchor” in my diagram was doing two jobs at once, and cell towers can replace only one of them.

What the anchor does
Transmit (illuminate the area). Cell towers can do this, so you don’t need your own transmitter.
Receive (listen and detect). Towers can’t do this for you. Something has to capture the tower’s direct signal as a reference, along with the tag reflections and any drone echoes. That’s where detection actually happens. The tags are passive reflectors and can’t report anything themselves. The tower’s own receivers belong to the operator and aren’t listening for any of this.
In the cell-signal version, the anchor shrinks to a receive-only listener node: a software-defined radio, antennas, timing and edge compute, about $1–3k. It has no transmitter, so it needs no FCC transmit authorisation.

Three ways to build it
Setup	Who transmits	Who receives	When to use it
A. Fully passive (your idea)	Existing cell towers	Your listener nodes	Default wherever cell coverage is strong
B. Hybrid	Towers, plus a small ISM-band illuminator of your own where needed	Your listener nodes	Weak coverage, or where tags need a legally clean signal
C. Operator-integrated (5G-Advanced/6G)	Operator base stations	Operator base stations	Later. You’d supply tags and software, with no hardware of your own beyond the tags.
Why you might still want your own transmitter in places
5G is bursty. LTE broadcasts reference signals continuously. 5G NR only sends short sync bursts when traffic is low (every ~20 ms by default), which leaves gaps in illumination at night or in quiet areas. LTE is the better illuminator while it lasts.
Geometry. You get whatever towers happen to be nearby. A single distant tower lights the site from one side at a low angle, leaving shadowed zones.
Regulation is the biggest issue. Tags that modulate an operator’s licensed signal effectively re-radiate in spectrum you don’t hold, and the FCC may see that as transmitting. Listeners that only receive are much clearer legally. Tags on your own ISM-band illuminator follow the UHF RFID precedent.
Control. Your own illuminator lets you pick the waveform, bandwidth and timing. Tower signals are limited to the operator’s carrier, which is typically 5–20 MHz on LTE and gives coarse range resolution.
What this means for the product
Default: fully passive listener nodes using LTE/5G. This does double duty: it’s passive radar for drone echoes at mid-altitude and forward-scatter detection on tower-to-listener lines.
Tags: add them where you need the low, cluttered final-approach tripwire. Pair them with a small ISM illuminator until the legal question about licensed-band tags is settled.
Long run: sell the tags as “sensing assist” reflectors to operators and venues once operator sensing is live, so the network itself becomes the receiver.

Partly. You can run a hardware demo in the spirit of this paper, but two parts of the plan won’t work as described: running your own LTE 450 base station, and using RFID tags to detect the drone.

Which paper this is. I couldn’t open the IEEE page, but this is almost certainly the Fraunhofer FKIE LTE450 passive-radar drone work (Demissie and Steffes); I read their 2025 open-access journal version. Their setup:

Transmitter: they didn’t run their own base station. They listened to two live base stations on a German utility network (450connect) about 10 km away, at 467 MHz with up to 5 MHz of bandwidth.
Receiver: a USRP X310 with TwinRX boards, and a four-Yagi array on a rooftop.
Processing: they used only the LTE reference signals, built a range-Doppler map and suppressed the direct signal from the tower.
Target: a DJI Matrice 210, about 5 kg, flying at about 30 m.
Results: reliable detection at about 500 m bistatic range, a weak peak at about 2.7 km, and an estimated 1.6 km limit. Detection probability was 75–96% with the array, with 2–3° azimuth error.
No tags at all. The drone wasn’t carrying anything; that’s the point of the paper. The GPS logger was only used afterwards to check the results.
Why the plan as stated doesn’t work

The US has no LTE 450 network to listen to. That band was used in Germany and a few other countries. Running your own eNodeB means you are transmitting, which needs an FCC licence (an FCC experimental licence, known as Part 5, for a university). That usually takes weeks to months. It also undercuts your “no transmitter, no licence” pitch.
RFID tags can’t detect a drone. Standard UHF RFID tags work at 902–928 MHz and are powered by an RFID reader, not by LTE signals. A tag on the drone would only find drones that carry your tag, and an attacker’s drone won’t.
What I’d demo instead: the same method, on US cell signals

Illuminator: a nearby commercial LTE tower in the 600–700 MHz bands. Those wavelengths behave much like 450 MHz, and listening-only is exactly your product.
Receiver options:
Two-channel coherent USRP B210 (about $2k). It can capture a full 10 MHz LTE carrier, which gives about 30 m range resolution.
KrakenSDR (about $600, five coherent channels). It’s limited to 2.4 MHz of bandwidth, which gives about 125 m range resolution.
Antennas: two directional antennas. One points at the tower to capture the reference signal; the other points at the area you’re watching.
Processing: the same steps as the paper (reference signals only, range-Doppler map, direct-signal cancellation), written in Python.
Ground truth: the DJI flight log.
Where tags are useful: calibration beacons, not detection

Place a few ambient-backscatter tags around the site. Each is a cheap RF switch and dipole toggling at a known rate, such as 200 Hz.
Each tag shows up in the range-Doppler map at a known position with a fake Doppler signature.
That proves the system works, calibrates the geometry, and is the “self-calibration” idea already in your pitch. It also makes a good live demo moment.
Staged plan with minimal risk

Week 1: record raw LTE signal data and get a clean direct-path peak.
Week 2: detect cars on a nearby road. They are large, easy targets.
Week 3: add the backscatter beacons.
Week 4: fly a drone. Use the largest drone you can get, or attach a small corner reflector for the first flights and say so honestly. A small DJI Mini-class drone will be detectable at much shorter range than the paper’s 5 kg Matrice.

Yes, Starlink can work as the signal source. Researchers in China reportedly used Starlink signals to detect a DJI Phantom 4 in a field test, and there are academic studies on Starlink for passive radar. But it behaves very differently from a cell tower, so treat it as a second mode rather than a replacement.

Where Starlink helps
It covers places cell towers don’t. If a remote base uses Starlink terminals, which many do, the satellites are already pointing beams at that exact spot. That is the illumination you need.
Fine range detail. Starlink channels are about 240 MHz wide, against 5–20 MHz for LTE, so in principle it can separate targets about a metre apart.
Good for telling drones from birds. At Ku-band (about 11–12 GHz), spinning propellers leave a strong signature in the signal, which helps tell drones apart from birds.
Cheap receivers. Ku-band front ends cost tens of dollars, because they are the same parts used in satellite TV dishes.
Where it’s harder
The signal is far weaker.
The satellite is about 550 km up, against about 1 km for a nearby cell tower. Even with its focused beam, the signal at the ground is much weaker, and the echo from a small drone is weaker still.
That’s why the Chinese test reportedly relied on forward scatter. In forward scatter you detect the drone when it crosses the line between the satellite and your receiver, rather than catching its reflection.
Forward scatter is great for a “crossing the fence” alert, which suits the Tripwire name, but it’s poor at measuring range.
The geometry keeps changing.
Each satellite moves at about 7.5 km/s and passes overhead in minutes, with beams that hop between areas.
You’d track satellites from public orbit data. Doable, but much more work than a fixed tower.
The signal format is proprietary.
Starlink’s signal is encrypted. Researchers have worked out its synchronisation structure, but you’d be building on reverse-engineered details, not a published standard like LTE.
Still receive-only, so the regulatory position is similar to LTE.
Remote airbases don’t need your main selling point.
Military sites have their own spectrum rights and already buy active radar such as Echodyne.
Your “no transmitter, no licence” advantage matters most at civil sites. Bases are also where Hidden Level already sells.
Other signal sources for sites without cell coverage
FM radio and digital TV. They reach tens of kilometres and are the best-studied passive-radar sources; HENSOLDT’s Twinvis uses them.
Geostationary satellite TV. Fixed geometry and stronger than Starlink, but too high-frequency and narrow for range detail.
Your own low-power transmitter on military sites. Allowed there, but it gives up the passive angle.
Private LTE/5G that bases deploy themselves. It turns back into your core case.

what is this?

CI stands for continuous integration. Every time someone pushes code or opens a pull request on GitHub, a service such as GitHub Actions automatically runs a set of checks on it:

Tests, for example confirming the simulated echo shows up at the right range and speed, or that a drone on a known path gets tracked correctly.
Linting and type checks, which catch sloppy or broken code.
A build, which confirms the app still compiles and starts.
If any check fails, the pull request shows a red ❌ and you fix it before merging into main. If everything passes, you get a green ✅.

Why it matters for your project:

Engineering practices are 25% of the grade. Automated tests on every pull request are exactly what graders look for.
It stops four people from breaking each other’s work. For example, if whoever owns the frontend changes something that breaks the detection pipeline, CI catches it before it lands.
It’s quick to set up. One YAML file in .github/workflows/ covers it, which is why it’s in the Week 7 “Foundations” box on the plan.
You’ll often see it paired with CD (continuous deployment): once checks pass on main, the app automatically redeploys to its public URL.
